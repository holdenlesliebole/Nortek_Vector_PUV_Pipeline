\section{Overview}
% ==================================================================

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Motivation}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Nearshore wave transformation governs beach morphology,
          sediment transport, and coastal hazards
    \item Accurate in-situ wave measurements at 5--15~m
          depth are needed to validate spectral wave models and drive
          transport calculations
    \item PUV (pressure--velocity) measurements from bottom-mounted ADVs
          provide directional spectra without surface moorings
    \item Goal: build a robust, reproducible processing pipeline from
          raw Nortek Vector data to validated wave statistics
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\note{
  The core problem: we need wave forcing at the depths where sediment
  actually moves, not just offshore buoy data. CDIP's MOP model gives
  us spectral predictions at 10~m depth, but we had no systematic way
  to validate those predictions or extract the higher-order products
  --- bed velocity, stresses, skewness --- that drive transport.
  PUV sensors on the bottom give us all three components
  (pressure + two horizontal velocities) without the mooring
  headaches of a surface buoy in the surfzone.
}

% ==================================================================
\section{L1 Processing}
% ==================================================================

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Raw Data Ingestion}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Nortek Vector burst files: \texttt{.dat} (data),
          \texttt{.sen} (sensor/diagnostics), \texttt{.hdr} (header)
    \item Parsed with MATLAB \texttt{textscan} for speed ---
          deployments can exceed $10^8$ samples
    \item Continuous 2~Hz time series reconstructed from
          burst boundaries
    \item \textbf{Clock drift correction}: linear interpolation
          between deployment and recovery clocks
    \item Battery cutoff detection: gaps $> 1~s$
          $\Rightarrow$ discard remainder of record
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\note{
  Each instrument produces three file types. The .dat file has the
  actual velocity and pressure samples, .sen has pitch/roll/heading
  and diagnostics, and .hdr is the configuration header. A single
  deployment can be 100 million samples at 2~Hz over several months,
  so parsing speed matters --- textscan is significantly faster than
  readtable for this. Clock drift is typically a few seconds over a
  3-month deployment; we correct it by linear interpolation between
  the deployment time and the recovery time recorded in the field
  notes. When the battery dies, we often see a gap longer than 1
  second, so everything after that gets discarded.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Quality Control --- Old vs.\ New Approach}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{Old: Fixed threshold}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Absolute $\pm 5^\circ$ pitch/roll cap
        \item Discards all tilted data
        \item Problematic when pipes bend during storms ---
              stable but non-zero tilt is \emph{correctable}
      \end{itemize}
      \vspace{0.5em}
      \textbf{New: Variability-based QC}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Rolling std of pitch/roll over 60~s windows
        \item Flag samples where variability $> 2^\circ$
        \item Absolute cap retained at $30^\circ$ for
              severely compromised instruments
        \item Stable tilts \emph{retained} and corrected
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{TBR23_MOP580_7m_L1_QC.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize L1 QC diagnostic for TBR23 MOP580 at 8.3~m depth.
      Top two panels: pitch and roll time series (blue) with QC flags (red).
      Third panel: rolling standard deviation of tilt with the
      $2^\circ$ variability threshold (dashed). Bottom: pressure
      record confirming continuous data collection.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  The old approach threw out any data where the instrument tilted more
  than 5 degrees. The problem is that storm waves can bend the
  mounting pipe, so the instrument sits at a stable 8 or 10 degree
  tilt for the rest of the deployment. That tilt is constant and
  correctable --- it's not noise. Our new approach looks at the
  variability of pitch and roll in 60-second windows. If the
  instrument is rocking back and forth by more than 2 degrees, that's
  real instability and we flag it. But a steady tilt gets retained
  and corrected in the next step. You can see in this diagnostic plot
  that pitch and roll are stable throughout this deployment --- the
  variability stays well below the threshold.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Tilt Correction}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      Sample-by-sample 3D rotation using measured pitch ($\phi$) and
      roll ($\theta$):
      %
      \begin{equation*}
        \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{R}_\text{roll}(\theta)\;
        \mathbf{R}_\text{pitch}(\phi)
      \end{equation*}
      %
      \begin{equation*}
        = \begin{bmatrix}
            1 & 0 & 0 \\
            0 & \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\
            0 & \sin\theta &  \cos\theta
          \end{bmatrix}
          \begin{bmatrix}
            \cos\phi & 0 & \sin\phi \\
            0 & 1 & 0 \\
            -\sin\phi & 0 & \cos\phi
          \end{bmatrix}
      \end{equation*}
      %
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Applied \emph{before} heading rotation to buoy
              coordinates
        \item Corrects velocity components for instrument tilt
        \item Recovers data from bent mounting pipes (post-storm)
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{tilt_correction_example.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize U--W scatter for SOL23 MOP651 (7~m) with
      pitch~$= -5.8^\circ$, roll~$= 8.7^\circ$ (bent pipe).
      (a)~Before correction: slope $= -0.084$, matching
      $\sin(\text{tilt}) = -0.100$ (dotted). (b)~After rotation:
      slope reduced to $+0.053$.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  This is a straightforward Euler rotation --- we apply the roll
  matrix first, then pitch. The key detail is that this happens
  before we rotate into geographic coordinates using the compass
  heading. For instruments on bent pipes, this correction removes
  a systematic bias in the vertical velocity component. Without it,
  a 10-degree tilt projects about 17 percent of the horizontal
  velocity into the vertical, which contaminates Reynolds stress
  and TKE estimates. The correction is sample-by-sample, not
  burst-averaged, because tilt can change within a burst during
  storm conditions.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Coordinate Rotation}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Instrument XYZ $\rightarrow$ geographic buoy coordinates
        \item Convention: $+x$ West, $+y$ North, $+z$ Up
              (left-handed, MOP-consistent)
        \item Heading from deployment notes $+$ magnetic declination
              via IGRF-13 model at deployment location and epoch
        \item Shore-normal rotation applied later in L2 using CDIP
              THREDDS shore-normal angle for each MOP station
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{TBR23_MOP580_7m_mean_direction.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize Mean wave direction vs.\ frequency for TBR23
      MOP580 at 8.3~m. Red: MOP model (10~m). Blue: PUV
      observation (8.3~m). Dashed: shore-normal angle
      ($264^\circ$). PUV direction converges toward shore-normal
      at swell frequencies, consistent with refraction.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  After tilt correction, we rotate from instrument coordinates to
  geographic coordinates using the compass heading and magnetic
  declination from the IGRF-13 model. The convention follows MOP:
  +x west, +y north, +z up. This is a left-handed coordinate system,
  which matches what CDIP uses. The figure shows mean wave direction
  as a function of frequency --- the PUV direction at swell
  frequencies converges toward the shore-normal, exactly what you'd
  expect from refraction bringing waves in toward the coast.
  The shore-normal rotation happens later in L2, where we pull the
  angle from CDIP's THREDDS server for each MOP station.
}

% ==================================================================
\section{L2 Spectral Analysis}
% ==================================================================

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Segmentation}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Segment length}: 2048 samples = 17.1~min at 2~Hz
    \item Non-overlapping segments
    \item \textbf{NaN policy}: segments with $>$10\% NaN discarded;
          $\leq$10\% filled by linear interpolation
    \item Segment-mean pressure $\rightarrow$ water depth $H$
    \item All channels linearly detrended before spectral analysis
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\footnotesize
  \textbf{Typical deployment yield}: TBR23 at 8.3~m depth
  $\rightarrow$ 5228 valid segments over 63 days
  ($\approx$83 segments/day, 99.7\% fill rate).}
\end{frame}

\note{
  Each segment is 2048 samples, which at 2 Hz gives us 17.1 minutes.
  That's long enough to get stable spectral estimates but short
  enough to track tidal modulation of depth. Non-overlapping because
  we want independent estimates. The NaN policy is practical ---
  a few dropped samples from a communication glitch get interpolated,
  but if more than 10 percent of a segment is missing, we throw it
  out. Detrending removes any slow pressure drift or mean current
  from the spectra. For a typical deployment like TBR23 at 8.3 m,
  we get about 5200 segments over two months, so the fill rate
  is excellent.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Spectral Estimation}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Method}: Welch's averaged periodogram
        \item Sub-segments: $N_\text{fft} = 256$ samples (128~s)
        \item \textbf{Window}: Hanning, 50\% overlap
        \item $\approx$\textbf{34 effective DOF} per segment
        \item $\Delta f \approx 0.0078~\text{Hz}$,
              range 0--1~Hz
        \item \textbf{Cross-spectra}: $S_{pu}$, $S_{pv}$ for
              directional coefficients $a_1$, $b_1$
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{TBR23_MOP580_7m_spectral_overlay.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize Six example segments from TBR23 MOP580 (8.3~m).
      Blue: PUV surface elevation spectrum $S_\eta(f)$ after
      pressure correction. Red: MOP shoaled to PUV depth.
      Vertical dashed lines mark $f_\text{cut}$ where $K_p < 0.1$.
      Note the consistent excess at the swell peak (0.06--0.08~Hz).}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  The spectral estimation is standard Welch with Hanning windows and
  50\% overlap. With 256-sample sub-segments inside each 2048-sample
  segment, we get about 34 effective degrees of freedom, which gives
  reasonably tight confidence intervals. The figure shows six example
  spectra comparing PUV against MOP. The key thing to notice is the
  swell peak --- in almost every panel, the PUV spectrum (blue) sits
  above the MOP spectrum (red) right at the peak frequency, around
  0.06 to 0.08 Hz. That's the bias that motivated our hypothesis
  testing. Also note the high-frequency cutoff: above about 0.3 Hz,
  we zero out the spectrum because the pressure correction factor
  $K_p$ drops below 0.1 and the signal is dominated by noise.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Pressure Correction}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      Surface elevation spectrum from pressure:
      %
      \begin{equation*}
        S_\eta(f) = \frac{S_p(f)}{K_p^2(f)}, \qquad
        K_p(f) = \frac{\cosh(k\, z_s)}{\cosh(k\, H)}
      \end{equation*}
      %
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Wavenumber $k(f)$: linear dispersion relation
              $\omega^2 = g k \tanh(kH)$ solved via Newton's method
        \item \textbf{Cutoff}: $K_p < 0.1$ $\Rightarrow$ set
              $S_\eta(f) = 0$ (noise suppression)
        \item Resulting $f_\text{cut}$ recorded per segment
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{wavenumber_comparison_H8m.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize Wavenumber $k(f)$ at 8~m depth from three
      formulations: Newton's method (baseline), Wu (1986)
      approximation, and O'Reilly coefficients. All three
      produce identical results, confirming that the pressure
      correction is not sensitive to the wavenumber solver.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  The pressure correction is the most important step in PUV processing.
  At the bottom, pressure fluctuations from surface waves are
  attenuated exponentially with depth, so we need to amplify the
  measured pressure spectrum to recover the surface spectrum. The
  transfer function $K_p$ depends on the wavenumber, which we get
  from the linear dispersion relation solved by Newton's method.
  A natural concern is whether the wavenumber solver introduces bias.
  The figure shows that three independent formulations give identical
  results, so the solver is not the issue. The cutoff at $K_p = 0.1$
  prevents noise amplification at high frequencies where the pressure
  signal is essentially zero.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Bulk Wave Parameters}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item $H_s = 4\sqrt{m_0}$, computed separately for:
          \begin{itemize}
            \item Sea--swell: 0.04--0.25~Hz
            \item Infragravity: 0.004--0.04~Hz
          \end{itemize}
        \item Peak period $T_p = 1/f_p$ (max $S_\eta$ in SS band)
        \item Mean period $T_{m02} = \sqrt{m_0/m_2}$
        \item Mean direction from $a_1$, $b_1$ (PUV cross-spectra)
        \item Energy flux
              $F = \rho g \int c_g(f)\, S_\eta(f)\, df$
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{annotated_spectrum_example.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize Example $S_\eta(f)$ from TBR23 MOP580 (8.3~m).
      Dashed lines mark IG/sea-swell band boundaries (0.04, 0.25~Hz).
      Shaded area: $m_0$ integral. Arrows: $f_p$ ($T_p = 14.2$~s).
      Red dotted: $f_\text{cut}$ where $K_p < 0.1$.
      $H_s = 4\sqrt{m_0} = 0.77$~m.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  These are the standard bulk wave parameters. $H_s$ is four times
  the square root of the zeroth moment of the spectrum, computed
  separately for sea-swell and infragravity bands. The cutoff between
  IG and sea-swell is 0.04 Hz, which is about 25 seconds. Peak period
  comes from the frequency of maximum spectral density in the
  sea-swell band. Direction comes from the first-order Fourier
  coefficients $a_1$ and $b_1$, which we get from the
  pressure-velocity cross-spectra. Energy flux integrates the
  spectrum weighted by group velocity at each frequency.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Additional L2 Products}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.45\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Near-bed velocity}: IFFT method ---
              scale velocity FFT by
              $\cosh(kH)/\cosh[k(H - z_s)]$
        \item \textbf{Bed shear stress}: Swart (1974)
              friction factor, $k_s = 2.5\, D_{50}$
        \item \textbf{Reynolds stress}: $\overline{u'w'}$,
              $\overline{v'w'}$; turbulent KE
        \item \textbf{Velocity moments}: skewness $S_k$,
              asymmetry $A_s$ (Elgar 1985)
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.53\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{TBR23_MOP580_7m_L2_verification.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize L2 product verification for TBR23 MOP580 (8.3~m).
      Top row: bed velocity IFFT vs.\ spectral theory (1:1),
      bed stress vs.\ orbital velocity, TKE vs.\ $H_s$.
      Middle: skewness and asymmetry distributions.
      Bottom: cross-shore and alongshore mean currents,
      energy flux vs.\ $H_s$.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  Beyond bulk wave parameters, we extract several higher-order
  products that matter for transport. The near-bed velocity uses an
  inverse FFT method --- we take the measured velocity spectrum, apply
  a depth transfer function to project it to the bed, then inverse
  transform to get a time series. The bed stress uses Swart's 1974
  friction factor with roughness tied to grain size. The verification
  plot shows all these products checking out: bed velocity IFFT matches
  spectral theory on a 1:1 line, the skewness distribution is
  centered slightly positive as expected for shoaling waves, and
  energy flux scales with $H_s$ squared as it should.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Shore-Normal Rotation}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Shore-normal angle retrieved from CDIP THREDDS for each
          MOP station
    \item Velocities rotated: $+x$ onshore, $+y$ alongshore north
    \item \textbf{Fallback}: if THREDDS unavailable, retain
          geographic buoy coordinates ($+x$ W, $+y$ N)
    \item Shore-normal frame required for cross-shore energy flux
          and transport calculations
  \end{itemize}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\footnotesize
  Example: MOP580 shore-normal = $264^\circ$ (facing WSW).
  After rotation, positive $u$ = onshore (toward $084^\circ$),
  positive $v$ = alongshore toward north.}
\end{frame}

\note{
  The shore-normal rotation is necessary because transport
  calculations need cross-shore and alongshore components, not
  geographic east-north. We pull the shore-normal angle from CDIP's
  THREDDS server for each MOP transect. At Torrey Pines, the
  shoreline faces roughly WSW, so the shore-normal is about 264
  degrees. After rotation, positive u means onshore and positive v
  means alongshore toward the north. This frame is what you need for
  computing undertow and longshore currents.
}

\section{Validation Against MOP}
% ==================================================================

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{MOP Wave Model}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item CDIP Monitoring and Prediction (MOP) spectral wave model
        \item Initialized by offshore CDIP buoy observations
        \item Provides $S_\eta(f)$ at 10~m depth, hourly
        \item \textbf{Shoaling to PUV depth}:
      \end{itemize}
      \begin{equation*}
        S_\eta(f,\, h_\text{PUV}) = S_\eta(f,\, 10\,\text{m})
        \times \frac{c_g(f,\, 10\,\text{m})}{c_g(f,\, h_\text{PUV})}
      \end{equation*}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item PUV segments (17~min) interpolated to MOP hourly
              time base for comparison
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize Top: time-averaged PUV spectrum (blue) vs.\ MOP
      shoaled to 8.3~m (red) for TBR23 MOP580. Bottom: spectral
      difference after normalizing each spectrum to percent of total
      energy. Blue shading marks frequencies where PUV carries a
      larger share of energy; red where MOP does.}

\note{
  The validation reference is CDIP's MOP model. MOP ingests offshore
  buoy spectra and propagates them shoreward using linear wave theory.
  It outputs spectra at 10~m depth, so we shoal those to the PUV
  instrument depth using the group velocity ratio. The spectral
  excess plot on the right is the key diagnostic that started this
  whole investigation --- when you subtract the MOP spectrum from the
  PUV spectrum, there's a clear positive excess right at the swell
  peak, around 0.06 to 0.08 Hz. That excess is what drives the
  positive $H_s$ bias we'll quantify next.

  One limitation to keep in mind: MOP propagates energy forward from
  offshore and cannot account for energy reflected off the beach back
  seaward. PUVs at 5--8~m depth measure the superposition of incident
  and reflected waves. At low frequencies (IG band), this reflection
  can be substantial --- free IG waves generated at the breakpoint
  reflect efficiently off the shoreline. MOP has no mechanism for this,
  so any reflected energy appears as ``excess'' in the PUV--MOP comparison.
  Distinguishing incident from reflected energy at a single cross-shore
  location requires the L4 cross-shore array analysis.
}

\section{Hypothesis Testing}
% ==================================================================

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Overview: Four Hypotheses}
  What causes the systematic positive $H_s$ bias (PUV $>$ MOP)?
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Nonlinear shoaling} --- triad interactions
          (Ursell number)
    \item \textbf{Directional narrowing} --- refraction-driven
          focusing
    \item \textbf{Bound long waves} --- group-forced IG
          contamination
    \item \textbf{MOP spectral peak broadening} --- numerical
          diffusion in the wave model
  \end{enumerate}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  Each hypothesis predicts a specific correlation between
  a physical parameter and the observed $H_s$ amplification
  ratio (PUV/MOP).
\end{frame}

\note{
  We tested four hypotheses for the bias. Each one predicts a
  different mechanism and a different diagnostic correlation. The
  approach is systematic: for each hypothesis, we identify the
  relevant physical parameter, compute it for all 5200+ segments, and
  test whether it correlates with the amplification ratio. Three of
  these turn out to be dead ends; one is supported. Let me walk
  through each one.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{H1: Nonlinear Shoaling}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Prediction}: nonlinear triad interactions
              during shoaling transfer energy to harmonics,
              increasing $H_s$ beyond linear theory
        \item \textbf{Test}: correlate spectral ratio with Ursell
              number $\mathit{Ur} = H_s L_p^2 / h^3$
        \item $N = 5228$ segment-hours
        \item \textbf{Result}: $R^2 = 0.004$
        \item Median $\mathit{Ur}$: 12 at 8.3~m,
              30 at 5.5~m
        \item[$\Rightarrow$] \alert{RULED OUT}
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{TBR23_ursell_vs_ratio.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize Spectral ratio (PUV/MOP in swell band) vs.\ Ursell
      number for 5228 hourly segments. No correlation
      ($R^2 = 0.004$). If nonlinear shoaling drove the bias, higher
      $Ur$ would predict larger ratios.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  Hypothesis 1: nonlinear shoaling. The idea is that as waves shoal
  into shallow water, triad interactions transfer energy from the
  primary peak to harmonics, so the actual wave height exceeds what
  linear theory predicts. The Ursell number quantifies how nonlinear
  the wave field is. If this were the explanation, we'd see a positive
  correlation between Ursell number and the amplification ratio.
  The scatter plot shows absolutely nothing --- $R^2$ of 0.004. The
  bias is just as large when Ursell is 5 as when it's 50. Ruled out.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{H2: Directional Narrowing}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Prediction}: refraction narrows directional
              spread during shoaling $\Rightarrow$ energy focusing
              $\Rightarrow$ higher $H_s$
        \item \textbf{Result}: PUV spread is systematically
              \emph{wider} than MOP at swell frequencies
              ($15^\circ$ vs.\ $10^\circ$)
        \item Opposite to prediction
        \item Correlation with $H_s$ amplification:
              $R^2 < 0.001$
        \item[$\Rightarrow$] \alert{RULED OUT}
      \end{itemize}
      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\footnotesize Wider PUV spread may reflect scattered energy
      at shallow depth not captured by the forward-propagating
      MOP model. MOP directional spread is expected to have a
      lower limit set by its angular resolution and propagation
      scheme --- confirming this floor is an open item.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{TBR23_MOP580_7m_spread_comparison.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize Top: mean directional spread vs.\ frequency.
      Red: MOP (10~m), Blue: PUV (8.3~m). PUV spread is wider
      at all swell frequencies. Bottom: spread ratio (MOP/PUV);
      values $>$1 mean PUV is wider. If directional narrowing drove
      the bias, PUV would be \emph{narrower} (ratio $<$1).}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  Hypothesis 2: directional narrowing from refraction. As waves shoal,
  refraction bends them toward shore-normal, which should narrow the
  directional spread and focus energy. If that focusing isn't captured
  by MOP's linear shoaling, it could explain the bias. But the data
  show the opposite: PUV directional spread is systematically wider
  than MOP, about 15 degrees versus 10 degrees at swell frequencies.
  The bottom panel shows the spread ratio is above 1 everywhere,
  meaning PUV is less focused, not more. There's also no correlation
  between the spread ratio and the $H_s$ amplification. The wider
  PUV spread probably reflects scattered energy from bottom
  irregularities and refraction over the real bathymetry, which the
  forward-propagating MOP model doesn't capture.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{H3: Bound Long Waves}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Prediction}: bound wave energy
              $\propto H_s^2$ (Longuet-Higgins \& Stewart 1962)
              leaks into the swell band
        \item \textbf{Result}: no positive correlation with
              $H_s^2$ ($R = -0.14$)
        \item Excess in \emph{wrong frequency band} ---
              at swell peak (0.04--0.08~Hz), not at group
              frequencies (0.01--0.03~Hz)
        \item[$\Rightarrow$] \alert{RULED OUT}
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{TBR23_MOP580_7m_bound_wave_scaling.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize Swell-band excess energy (PUV $-$ MOP) vs.\ $H_s^2$.
      If bound long waves were responsible, excess energy would scale
      positively with $H_s^2$. Instead, correlation is weakly
      negative ($R = -0.14$). The excess also appears at swell
      frequencies, not IG frequencies where bound waves reside.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  Hypothesis 3: bound long waves. These are forced infragravity
  oscillations that travel locked to wave groups. Their energy
  scales with $H_s$ squared. If some of that energy leaked into
  the swell band --- maybe through spectral leakage or nonlinear
  interactions --- it could explain the bias. Two problems: first,
  the correlation with $H_s$ squared is actually slightly negative,
  not positive. Second, the excess energy appears at the swell peak
  around 0.06 Hz, not at the group frequencies below 0.03 Hz where
  bound waves live. Wrong amplitude scaling, wrong frequency band.
  Ruled out.

  Note that this doesn't rule out \emph{free} IG waves reflected off
  the beach, which is a separate mechanism. MOP treats the shoreline
  as a sink and can't represent reflected energy propagating back
  seaward past the PUV. A single cross-shore PUV can't decompose
  incident vs.\ reflected energy --- that requires the L4 array work.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{H4: MOP Spectral Peak Broadening}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item $Q_p$ (Goda peakedness): PUV \textbf{24\%} higher
              than MOP (multi-taper processing)
        \item Previous Welch estimate showed only 7\% ---
              Welch was smoothing the PUV peak too
        \item $\Delta Q_p$ vs.\ $H_s$ amplification:
              $R = 0.46$, $R^2 = 0.21$
        \item[$\Rightarrow$] \textbf{\color{green!60!black}SUPPORTED}
      \end{itemize}
      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\footnotesize MOP model introduces numerical broadening of the
      spectral peak during propagation. Multi-taper processing
      resolves the true PUV peak shape, revealing a larger peakedness
      gap than Welch estimated.}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize $Q_p = \frac{2}{m_0^2}\int f\,S_\eta^2\,df$
      (Goda, 1970). Higher $Q_p$ = sharper, more peaked spectrum.}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{TBR23_MOP580_7m_normalized_shape.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize Frequency-normalized spectral shape
      (centered on $f_p$, normalized by peak density).
      Blue: PUV. Red: MOP. The PUV peak is taller and narrower;
      MOP spreads energy laterally. This shape difference
      accounts for the integrated $H_s$ bias.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  Hypothesis 4, and this is the one that works: MOP spectral peak
  broadening. When we normalize the spectra by their peak frequency
  and peak density, the shape difference is obvious --- PUV has a
  taller, narrower peak, while MOP spreads that energy across a
  wider frequency range. With multi-taper processing ($NW = 4$,
  nfft = 2048), the Goda peakedness $Q_p$ shows PUV spectra are
  24\% more peaked than MOP --- our original Welch estimate of 7\%
  was understating the difference because Welch was also smoothing
  the PUV peak. $Q_p$ is defined as
  $2/m_0^2 \int f\,S_\eta^2\,df$; it equals 1 for white noise and
  2--4 for narrow-banded swell. The correlation between the
  peakedness difference and $H_s$ amplification is $R = 0.46$
  ($R^2 = 0.21$). MOP propagates spectra through a numerical grid,
  introducing frequency-space diffusion. The real ocean preserves
  the narrow swell peak shape.
}

\section{L3 Forcing Characterization}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{L3 Architecture}
  L3 transforms L2 spectra into forcing metrics relevant to sediment
  transport. Four sub-modules, each deployment-agnostic:
  \vspace{0.5em}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{L3a: Band Decomposition}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item IG: 0.004--0.04~Hz
        \item Swell: 0.04--0.10~Hz
        \item Sea: 0.10--0.25~Hz
        \item $E_f$ and $H_s$ per band, per segment
      \end{itemize}
      \vspace{0.5em}
      \textbf{L3b: Storm Detection}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Threshold exceedance + duration
        \item MOP gap-filling for instrument failures
        \item Event metrics: peak $H_s$, duration, cumulative $F$
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \textbf{L3c: Transport Proxies}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Bottom energy flux $F_b$
        \item Shields parameter $\theta_s$
        \item Rouse number $R_o$
        \item Mobilization fraction (\% time $> \theta_{cr}$)
      \end{itemize}
      \vspace{0.5em}
      \textbf{L3d: Current Decomposition}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Tidal harmonics (\texttt{t\_tide})
        \item Subtidal (wave-driven) residual
        \item Undertow + longshore current
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vspace{0.5em}
  {\footnotesize Energy conservation verified:
  $E_{f,\text{total}} / E_{f,\text{L2}} = 1.0000$ for all 33
  instruments (no energy created or lost in band partitioning).}
\end{frame}

\note{
  L3 takes the spectral output from L2 and computes everything we
  need to characterize wave forcing for sediment transport. It's
  organized into four sub-modules. L3a partitions energy into
  infragravity, swell, and sea bands. L3b detects storm events from
  the $H_s$ time series, and this is where MOP gap-filling is
  critical --- when an instrument fails during a storm, we use MOP
  to fill in what happened. L3c computes transport proxies: Shields
  parameter, Rouse number, bottom energy flux. L3d decomposes
  currents into tidal and subtidal components. A key verification:
  when we sum the band-partitioned energy fluxes, they match the L2
  total to four decimal places. No energy created or lost.
}

\section{L4 and L5: Planned Extensions}
% ==================================================================

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{L4: Infragravity and Bound Wave Dynamics}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \textbf{Objective}: Separate forced (bound) and free
      infragravity waves using the co-located pressure and
      velocity measurements.
      \vspace{0.5em}
      \textbf{Approach}:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Bispectral analysis --- bicoherence at swell-IG
              coupling frequencies identifies energy transfer
              from swell triads to the IG band
        \item Phase relationship between IG pressure and wave
              group envelope distinguishes bound (locked phase)
              from free (random phase) IG waves
        \item IG energy flux and direction from $S_{pu}$ and
              $S_{pv}$ cross-spectra in the IG band
        \item Reflection coefficient from shoreward/seaward
              energy flux ratio
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
      \textbf{Why it matters}:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item IG waves dominate inner-surfzone runup
              and overwash
        \item Bound IG energy transfers to free waves during
              breaking --- the ratio controls IG amplitude
              at the shoreline
        \item MOP does not model IG waves; PUV measures them
              directly
        \item Multi-instrument arrays (TOR23W, TOR24) enable
              cross-shore IG coherence analysis
      \end{itemize}
      \vspace{0.5em}
      {\footnotesize Reference: Herbers et al.\ (1995);
      implementation informed by Athina Lange's Ruby2D code.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  L4 is about infragravity dynamics. IG waves are forced by wave
  groups through the radiation stress mechanism described by
  Longuet-Higgins and Stewart. In deep water they're bound to the
  groups, but during breaking the bound wave gets released as a free
  wave that can reflect off the shoreline. The bound-to-free ratio
  determines how much IG energy reaches the shoreline, which matters
  for runup and overwash. Our PUV instruments measure pressure and
  velocity in the IG band, and we have multi-instrument arrays at
  Torrey Pines that let us look at cross-shore coherence. Bispectral
  analysis quantifies the coupling between the swell peak and the IG
  band. This connects to Paper 1 because the IG band showed up in
  our spectral ratio analysis --- remember that the IG amplification
  was large but didn't correlate with the swell-band bias.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{L5: PUV--Altimeter Integration}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \textbf{Objective}: Correlate wave forcing (L2/L3 products)
      with bed-level change from co-located acoustic altimeters.
      \vspace{0.5em}
      \textbf{Data sources}:
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Aquatec AA400 altimeters (acoustic bed tracking)
        \item Simrad EA400 echologgers
        \item Co-located with PUV instruments at multiple
              Torrey Pines and Solana Beach sites
      \end{itemize}
      \vspace{0.5em}
      \textbf{Analysis plan}:
      \begin{enumerate}
        \item Time-align altimeter bed level with L2/L3 wave
              forcing segments
        \item Test 8 transport relationships: $F_b$, $\theta_s$,
              Shields exceedance, $H_s$, $S_k \cdot U_b^3$,
              storm cumulative flux, tidal phase, event-scale
              response
        \item Estimate transport rates from bed-level change
              + continuity equation
      \end{enumerate}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
      \textbf{Key question}: Which wave forcing metric best
      predicts observed bed-level change?
      \vspace{0.5em}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Is it total energy ($F_b$)?
        \item Is it the nonlinear shape ($S_k \cdot U_b^3$)?
        \item Does it depend on depth or grain size?
      \end{itemize}
      \vspace{0.5em}
      \textbf{Feedback to L2}: Time-varying bed level from
      altimeter corrects the pressure sensor offset
      (\texttt{doffp}), improving $K_p$ accuracy as
      sediment accretes or erodes around the instrument.
      \vspace{0.5em}
      {\footnotesize Starting point: TOR24S MOP586 (co-located,
      multi-depth array). See
      \texttt{docs/altimeter\_correlation\_plan.md}.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  L5 is the integration between PUV wave data and acoustic altimeter
  bed-level measurements. We have co-located instruments at several
  sites, and the question is straightforward: which wave forcing
  metric best predicts how the bed moves? We'll test eight different
  relationships --- from simple energy flux to nonlinear
  skewness-velocity products. There's also a feedback loop: the
  altimeter tells us how the bed level changes over time, and that
  bed-level change affects the pressure sensor height above the
  bottom, which in turn affects the pressure correction. So L5
  actually improves L2 accuracy. We're starting with TOR24S at
  MOP586, which has a multi-depth array of co-located PUV and
  altimeter instruments. The full analysis plan is documented in
  the repo.
}

\begin{frame}{Competing Transport Mechanisms}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      Two processes act simultaneously:
      \vspace{0.5em}

      \textbf{Onshore: velocity skewness} ($S_k > 0$)
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Peaked wave crests produce stronger onshore
              orbital velocities than troughs
        \item Bedload transport scales as $U_b^3$ weighted
              by skewness
        \item Drives recovery (shoreward sand movement)
      \end{itemize}
      \vspace{0.5em}

      \textbf{Offshore: undertow}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Wave-driven mean return flow below trough level
        \item Opposes recovery
        \item Strengthens with increasing $H_s$
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{/Users/holden/Documents/Scripps/Research/Paper_1/paper/figures/fig_transport_mechanisms_v4.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize Cross-shore transport decomposition for TBR23
      instruments. Skewness-driven flux (blue, onshore) vs.\
      undertow-driven flux (red, offshore). At 5.5~m depth,
      skewness dominates; at 8.3~m, the two are roughly balanced.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  The transport story has two competing players. Velocity skewness
  produces an asymmetry in the orbital velocities --- the crests are
  faster and shorter than the troughs, so bedload transport is biased
  onshore. Undertow is the mean offshore return flow driven by wave
  breaking. Both are present at every instrument, every segment. The
  net transport direction depends on which one wins. The figure shows
  the decomposition: at the 5.5-meter instruments, skewness transport
  is 3 to 4 times stronger than undertow, so the net flux is
  onshore, which is recovery. At 8.3 meters, the two are roughly
  balanced, so the net flux is near zero.
}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Recovery Boundary at 6--7~m Depth}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item At 5.5~m: skewness flux 3--4$\times$ stronger
              than undertow $\rightarrow$ net onshore (recovery)
        \item At 8.3~m: undertow slightly dominates
              $\rightarrow$ near-equilibrium
        \item Crossover at $\sim$6--7~m depth
        \item Explains: mid-July recovery pulse at 5~m site
              but not at 7~m sites
        \item Tidal modulation provides depth range at each
              fixed instrument
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{recovery_boundary_transport.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize Transport proxy decomposition for TBR23. Blue:
      skewness flux $\langle u|u|^2 \rangle$ (onshore). Red:
      undertow flux $\bar{u} \cdot U_b^2$ (offshore). Diamonds:
      net. At 5.5~m skewness dominates 4:1; at 8.3~m the two
      balance. Dashed lines: inferred 6--7~m crossover.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  The depth dependence creates a natural boundary around 6 to 7
  meters. Above that depth, skewness wins and sand moves onshore ---
  that's recovery. Below it, undertow roughly balances skewness and
  the profile stays near equilibrium. This explains a specific
  observation: in mid-July, the 5-meter instruments recorded a
  recovery pulse --- about 15 cm of accretion in two weeks --- but
  the 7-meter instruments saw essentially no change. The tidal range
  at Torrey Pines is about 1.5 meters, so each fixed instrument
  samples a range of effective depths over a tidal cycle, which
  gives us some depth resolution beyond just the two instrument
  depths.
}

\begin{frame}{Key Physics}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Skewness follows Ruessink et al.\ (2012) depth
              parameterization
          \begin{itemize}
            \item $S_k$ increases with decreasing $kh$
            \item Observed values match predicted curve
          \end{itemize}
        \item Undertow strengthens at high tide (more wave energy
              reaches instrument depth)
        \item Low tide reduces undertow, enabling onshore recovery
        \item MOP580\_5m excluded: kelp fouling produced artifact
              negative $S_k$
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
      \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{skewness_vs_ursell.png}

      \vspace{0.3em}
      {\scriptsize Velocity skewness vs.\ Ursell number for all
      TBR23 segments. Binned medians (black dots) from 3 clean
      instruments track the Ruessink et al.\ (2012) curve (red,
      surface-elevation $S_k$). Gray: kelp-fouled MOP580\_5m
      with anomalous negative skewness.}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

\note{
  Two pieces of physics anchor the interpretation. First, the
  skewness values we measure match the Ruessink et al.\ 2012
  parameterization, which predicts skewness as a function of the
  relative depth parameter $kh$. That gives us confidence that the
  PUV skewness measurements are physically reasonable, not artifacts.
  Second, there's a tidal modulation effect: at high tide, the water
  is deeper, so more wave energy reaches the instrument depth, which
  strengthens undertow and opposes recovery. At low tide, undertow
  weakens and onshore skewness transport can dominate. This tidal
  modulation of the transport balance is something we can quantify
  segment by segment with L3d's tidal decomposition. One
  instrument, MOP580 at 5 meters, was fouled by kelp during the
  deployment, which produced unphysical negative skewness values.
  We excluded it from the transport analysis.
}

\begin{frame}{Key Findings}
  \begin{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{TBR23 transport balance}: competing skewness

  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

  processed with no pipeline failures and strong MOP validation.
  Second, we found a systematic bias and traced it to the spectral
  peak. Third, the bias is a MOP model artifact, not a measurement
  problem --- MOP numerically broadens the spectral peak. Fourth,
  this isn't one site; it's across 11 instruments spanning seasons
  and depths. Fifth, L3 is fully operational, giving us transport
  proxies, storm detection, and tidal decomposition for all 33
  instruments. Sixth, the TBR23 analysis identified the depth-dependent
  competition between skewness and undertow, with a recovery boundary
  at 6 to 7 meters.
